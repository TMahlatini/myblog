### Thinking, Fast and Slow: Chapt 21

Speaking on [Paul Meehl's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_E._Meehl) *Clinical vs Statistical Prediction* research:  "The important conclusion from this research is that an algorithm that is constructed on the back of an envelope is often good enough to compete with an optimally weighted formula, and certainly good enough to outdo expert judgement." 

Another interesting quote: "The story of a child dying because an algorithm made a mistake is more poignant than a story of the same tragedy occurring as result of human error, and the difference in emotional intensity is readily translated into moral preference"

The latter quote reminds me of the movie *"I, Robot"*. There is a scene in there where a robot has to save two drowning people; one of them a 12 year old girl.  The robot calculated that the child had ~11% chance of survival and the adult ~45%. Trying to save both was off the table because they would most certainly both die. Based on cold hard math, the robot saved the protagonist even after he insisted on saving the child in his place. No matter how many times you watch it, it's hard to have sympathy for the robot's decision even though it was the *logical* thing to do. Ten years ago this movie scene was pure fiction, but now, some variation of this looks very likely in the near future.

Autonomous vehicles are now a thing: Waymo currently has ~4000 robotaxis and Tesla has started deploying cybercabs. These cars are going to be safer than human operated vehicles by a wide margin because human error accounts for almost all accidents. However, in this transitional period it's almost inevitable that there are going to be a couple accidents involving autonomous vehicles that future historians are going to look back at as the nexus in adoption or abandonment. Although I feel the latter is less likely, I am curios to see how it will all play out. All the sci-fi tropes are coming to life now.
